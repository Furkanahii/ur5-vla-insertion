Simulation-Based Vision–Language–Action Learning for Rigid and Compliant Peg-in-Hole Insertion Using NVIDIA Isaac Lab
Undergraduate research project brief  |  Two remote students  |  Proposed duration: 12 weeks
1. Objective and research question
Develop and evaluate a Vision–Language–Action (VLA) policy for robotic peg-in-hole insertion using NVIDIA Isaac Sim and Isaac Lab as the single simulation platform. Model the UR5 robot, a rigid peg and a rigid hole first; then investigate a rigid peg inserted into a deformable (soft) hole. Policies and simulation findings will subsequently be tested in physical insertion experiments with the laboratory UR5. No robot access is required during the initial remote phase.
Research question: Can a pretrained VLA model, adapted using simulated demonstrations, robustly align and insert a rigid peg under variations in initial position, orientation, and eventually hole compliance? What additional benefit does haptic (e.g. contact-force) information provide?
Scope clarification: Delivering a reproducible rigid-hole VLA training and evaluation pipeline is required. A physically plausible rigid–soft contact simulation is a separate feasibility milestone, not a guaranteed 12-week outcome. Human–robot dual-force learning and real haptic sensing are reserved for later research.

3. Simulation task and training pipeline
Task definition
Use a simulated UR5 robot with a securely modeled peg tool/gripper and a fixed insertion fixture. Start with a rigid hole and a consistent virtual RGB camera; document camera intrinsics/extrinsics, UR5 TCP and world/base/hole frames. Use an end-effector Cartesian action interface and a constrained low-level controller.
Randomize initial lateral offset and angular misalignment, then vary clearance, appearance, lighting and camera pose. Keep training and evaluation scenarios disjoint. Introduce hole stiffness/friction as additional parameters only after the soft-contact prototype is stable.
Use a constant language instruction initially: “Insert the peg into the hole.” Add instruction variation only after a stable vision-to-action baseline is established.
Demonstrations and model training
Generate expert demonstrations with a scripted pose-aware controller in Isaac Lab (or a compatible demonstration workflow). The expert may use simulator ground truth, but the trained VLA must not see hidden peg/hole poses except for an explicitly labeled oracle ablation.
Record synchronized RGB images, language command, UR5 joint/end-effector state, target Cartesian delta pose, peg–hole relative pose for evaluation, success/failure, and contact-state metadata. Record contact wrench only when a validated simulator readout is available.
Build and test an adapter from the shared episode schema to OpenVLA-OFT. Document action normalization, UR5 reference frames, control frequency, gripper/tool assumptions and action-chunking. Check coordinate transforms by replay before training.
Fine-tune the pretrained VLA and evaluate it in closed loop: prediction → low-level robot control → new observation. Teacher-forced prediction accuracy alone is not insertion success.

Implement success/contact checks; develop minimal rigid–soft contact feasibility test. | Compare to expert and non-VLA baselines; evaluate unseen starting poses.

Both students should jointly review sample episodes every week and keep one shared repository. Interfaces must be agreed upon before generating a large dataset.

Rigid–deformable feasibility gate (by week 4)
Before attempting soft-hole VLA training, run a prescribed-displacement test with a rigid cylindrical peg and a deformable elastomer block containing a cylindrical opening. Confirm actual hole deformation, nonpenetrating contact behavior, repeatable force/displacement output where accessible, and numerical stability at several step sizes or mesh resolutions. If this gate fails, retain rigid-hole VLA as the core deliverable and document the blocker rather than delaying the complete project.
6. Evaluation protocol and expected deliverables
For rigid-hole tests, compare (i) ground-truth pose scripted expert, (ii) a simpler vision-based controller or behavioral cloning baseline, and (iii) OpenVLA-OFT. On the same held-out configurations, optionally compare rigid-trained VLA to policies adapted with deformable-hole demonstrations. Force-free versus force-conditioned policies are a later experiment, conditional on trustworthy contact-force signals.
Primary metrics: insertion success rate over a fixed test set, completion time, final alignment error and jam/failure frequency.
Contact-rich metrics (where the simulator supports valid readouts): peak contact force/torque, force impulse, jam/recovery events and success across clearance and stiffness. If rigid–deformable forces are not directly available, report this explicitly; do not substitute an unvalidated estimate as ground truth.
Report results separately for seen versus unseen starting offsets/angles; where possible, repeat training with multiple seeds and show uncertainty.
Deliverables: reproducible repository and setup instructions, simulation/demo dataset and metadata, model checkpoints or training recipe, evaluation scripts, quantitative plots/tables, demonstration videos, and a short technical report.
7. Transition to physical validation on the UR5 robot
After the simulation phase, the success of the developed VLA policies will be evaluated through physical peg-in-hole experiments using a UR5 robot in the laboratory. These experiments are a required validation step, not part of the students’ initial remote-only work.
Build matching rigid peg/rigid hole fixtures first, followed by a rigid peg/compliant hole fixture with characterized geometry and stiffness.
Transfer the same Cartesian action/TCP convention from simulated UR5 to laboratory UR5. Match UR5 variant, gripper/tool geometry, controller constraints, hole fixture and camera placement as closely as practical; verify calibration and action scaling.
Calibrate the real camera and robot-to-camera transforms; check scale, action limits, time synchronization and end-effector pose tracking before learned-policy rollouts.
Run conservative supervised trials with collision checks, low speed/force limits and a tested emergency stop. Verify contact behavior before enabling autonomous insertion.
Compare physical and simulated success, time, alignment error, contact forces and jam behavior under matched initial offsets, angular errors and hole compliance. Quantify the simulation-to-reality (sim-to-real) gap.
If transfer is inadequate, quantify the sim-to-real gap and consider camera/lighting randomization, system identification, compliant-contact calibration, controller tuning and limited supervised real-robot adaptation. Physical validation must not be inferred from simulation success alone.
Hardware dependencies for the later UR5 phase: robot access; securely mounted insertion fixtures; calibrated camera; appropriate robot-side pose/control interface; and a compatible force/torque measurement method for haptic evaluation. The exact UR5 variant and sensing configuration will be confirmed before physical tests.
8. Future haptic learning extension
The broader research program includes simultaneous human–robot manipulation and two force measurements: human-applied force F_H and robot–object interaction force F_int. Under the proposal’s quasi-static assumptions and consistent sign convention, an estimate of environmental reaction force is F_env ≈ F_H − F_int, with dynamic, gravity and sensor-bias compensation when needed. These are later physical demonstration measurements, not data automatically available from a standard VLA.
During simulation, log contact state and deformation; log reaction wrench only if the rigid–deformable force extraction method has been implemented and checked. Isaac Lab standard contact-sensor support for rigid–deformable interactions may be limited. Before learning from wrench histories, validate force–displacement behavior and, later, compare with physical UR5 force measurements.
9. Success criteria and first assignment
Required outcome by week 12: a reproducible Isaac Lab UR5 rigid-hole benchmark, a demonstration dataset, an OpenVLA-OFT model running in closed loop, and quantitative comparisons to baselines. A separate required feasibility report should document rigid peg–deformable hole stability and force-access issues. A stable soft-hole task and force-conditioned VLA are stretch goals; physical UR5 results follow in the laboratory phase.
First assignment (weeks 1–2): Student A launches a UR5 asset in Isaac Lab, implements simple Cartesian motion and records a camera video plus pose/action log; Student B runs an official OpenVLA-OFT reference example and records software/GPU requirements. Jointly agree on a shared robot state/action and episode schema. By week 4, Student A must also show a separate minimal rigid peg–deformable hole simulation, without VLA, and document observed numerical stability.
